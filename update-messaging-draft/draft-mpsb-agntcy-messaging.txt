 An Overview of Messaging Systems and Their Applicability to Agentic AI
                     draft-mpsb-agntcy-messaging-03

Abstract

   Agentic AI systems require messaging infrastructure that supports
   real-time collaboration, high-volume streaming, and dynamic group
   coordination across distributed networks.  Traditional protocols like
   AMQP [AMQP], MQTT [MQTT], and NATS [NATS] address some requirements
   but fall short on security, particularly regarding post-compromise
   protection and forward secrecy essential for autonomous agents
   handling sensitive data.

   This document analyzes six messaging protocols—AMQP, MQTT, NATS, AMQP
   over WebSockets, Kafka, and AGNTCY SLIM—across dimensions critical
   for GenAI agent systems: streaming performance, delivery guarantees,
   security models, and operational complexity.  We examine how each
   protocol's design decisions impact agentic AI deployments, from
   lightweight edge computing scenarios to large-scale multi-
   organizational collaborations.

   This document also describes Agent Mesh [AgentMesh], an overlay for
   agent service discovery, policy enforcement, and cross-network
   connectivity.  It operates at the service-network layer, alongside
   messaging systems that provide message delivery and session
   semantics.

2.  Introduction

   When designing a multi-agent system for generative AI, the messaging
   layer becomes a critical piece of infrastructure.  GenAI agents—built
   with frameworks like LangGraph, AutoGen, or LlamaIndex—often need to
   collaborate in real time, exchange high volumes of streaming data
   (e.g., token-by-token outputs), and coordinate complex tasks such as
   voting or consensus.  Moreover, security requirements extend well
   beyond basic TLS; in scenarios where agents share sensitive models or
   partial computations, post-compromise security and robust end-to-end
   encryption are essential.

   In practice, a designer of such systems will require a protocol that
   efficiently handles one-to-many or many-to-many communication,
   supports dynamic membership (with agents joining or leaving on the
   fly), and scales to accommodate a “forest” of agents spread across
   global networks.  Some protocols excel at ultra-low-latency, high-
   throughput streaming—critical for continuous token streams or
   aggregated embeddings—while others emphasize strong consistency and
   durability.  Additionally, advanced cryptographic features such as
   automatic key rotation and forward secrecy are vital when compromised
   credentials must not enable an attacker to decrypt future
   communications.

   Below, we compare six messaging protocols—AMQP, MQTT, NATS, AMQP over
   WebSockets, Kafka, and AGNTCY SLIM (Secure Low-Latency Interactive
   Messaging)—across dimensions that matter for GenAI agent systems:
   streaming performance, delivery guarantees, flexible pub/sub
   patterns, agent coordination, security (including end-to-end
   encryption and zero-trust support), and real-world adoption.

3.  Protocol Analysis for Agentic AI Systems

   The following sections provide detailed analysis of each messaging
   protocol in the context of agentic AI requirements.

3.1.  Traditional Enterprise Messaging: AMQP

   The Advanced Message Queuing Protocol (AMQP), most commonly
   implemented through RabbitMQ, represents the gold standard for
   enterprise messaging systems.  AMQP's strength lies in its
   sophisticated message routing capabilities through exchanges, queues,
   and routing keys, enabling complex message flow patterns essential
   for enterprise applications.

   For agentic AI systems, AMQP offers several advantages.  Its support
   for both at-least-once and exactly-once delivery semantics
   (particularly in AMQP 1.0) ensures reliable message delivery between
   AI agents, which is crucial when agents are coordinating critical
   tasks or sharing expensive computational results.  The protocol's
   durable queue support means that agent messages can persist across
   system restarts, preventing loss of important coordination data.

   However, AMQP's enterprise focus comes with trade-offs.  The protocol
   carries higher overhead due to its rich feature set, which may impact
   performance in high-frequency agent communication scenarios.
   Streaming capabilities require extensions like RabbitMQ Streams,
   adding complexity to deployments focused on real-time agent
   collaboration.

   Authentication in AMQP relies on traditional enterprise mechanisms
   like SASL, LDAP, and Kerberos, which integrate well with existing
   corporate identity systems but may not align with modern cloud-native
   authentication patterns preferred in AI infrastructure.

3.2.  IoT-Optimized Messaging: MQTT

   Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) emerged from the IoT world
   with a focus on lightweight, efficient communication over constrained
   networks.  Its topic-based publish-subscribe model maps naturally to
   many agent communication patterns, where agents subscribe to topics
   representing different types of events or data streams.

   MQTT's three Quality of Service levels (QoS 0, 1, and 2) provide
   flexibility in balancing performance versus reliability.  For agentic
   AI systems, QoS 0 (at-most-once) works well for frequent status
   updates or non-critical notifications, while QoS 2 (exactly-once)
   ensures critical agent coordination messages are delivered reliably.

   The protocol's very low overhead makes it attractive for scenarios
   involving large numbers of lightweight AI agents or edge computing
   deployments where bandwidth is constrained.  However, MQTT's IoT
   heritage shows in its limitations for agentic AI use cases.  Native
   streaming support requires broker extensions, and message-level
   security typically relies entirely on transport-layer TLS rather than
   end-to-end encryption.

   MQTT's authentication mechanisms, while sufficient for IoT devices,
   may not provide the sophisticated identity and access management
   features required for complex multi-agent AI systems involving
   different trust domains.

3.3.  Cloud-Native Messaging: NATS

   NATS represents a modern approach to messaging designed for cloud-
   native architectures.  Its lightweight design and support for
   multiple communication patterns—publish-subscribe, request-reply, and
   queue groups—make it particularly well-suited for microservices-based
   AI agent deployments.

   The protocol's core at-most-once delivery semantics align well with
   scenarios where AI agents can tolerate occasional message loss in
   favor of high performance.  For use cases requiring stronger
   guarantees, NATS JetStream provides at-least-once delivery and
   streaming capabilities, though this requires additional
   infrastructure complexity.

   NATS's optional broker architecture offers interesting deployment
   flexibility for agentic AI systems.  While most deployments use a
   broker for efficiency, the protocol can support peer-to-peer
   communication, potentially enabling direct agent-to-agent
   communication in specialized scenarios.

   Authentication in NATS includes modern options like JWT tokens and
   NKey cryptographic authentication, aligning better with cloud-native
   security practices.  However, like MQTT, NATS relies primarily on
   transport-layer security rather than providing built-in end-to-end
   message encryption.

3.4.  Browser Integration: AMQP over WebSockets

   AMQP over WebSockets addresses a specific deployment challenge:
   enabling browser-based AI agents or user interfaces to participate in
   AMQP-based agent coordination systems.  This approach tunnels
   standard AMQP protocols through WebSocket connections, allowing web
   applications to overcome firewall restrictions and network topology
   limitations.

   For agentic AI systems that include web-based components—such as
   user-facing AI assistants that need to coordinate with backend AI
   agents—this protocol variant provides a bridge between browser
   environments and enterprise messaging infrastructure.  The WebSocket
   Secure (WSS) transport ensures encrypted communication from browser
   to broker.

   However, the additional protocol layers (AMQP within WebSockets)
   introduce higher overhead compared to native AMQP or other
   lightweight protocols.  This makes AMQP over WebSockets primarily
   suitable for scenarios where browser integration is essential rather
   than for high-performance agent-to-agent communication.

3.5.  High-Throughput Streaming: Apache Kafka

   Apache Kafka [Kafka] represents a fundamentally different approach to
   messaging, based on distributed commit logs rather than traditional
   message queues.  This architecture provides exceptional throughput
   and built-in streaming capabilities that align well with certain
   agentic AI use cases.

   Kafka's partition-based topic model enables massive horizontal
   scaling, making it suitable for AI systems that need to process large
   volumes of training data, model updates, or inference results across
   distributed agent networks.  The platform's native streaming
   capabilities through Kafka Streams and ksqlDB provide powerful tools
   for real-time processing of agent-generated data.

   The protocol's built-in persistence across distributed clusters
   ensures that agent communication history is preserved and can be
   replayed, which is valuable for AI systems that need to audit agent
   decisions or retrain models based on historical interactions.
   Consumer groups enable multiple agents to process different
   partitions of the same topic concurrently, supporting parallel AI
   workloads.

   However, Kafka's strengths come with complexity costs.  The
   requirement for a distributed cluster infrastructure may be overkill
   for simpler agent coordination tasks.  While Kafka provides exactly-
   once semantics through transactions, the default at-least-once
   delivery may require additional deduplication logic in agent
   implementations.

   Kafka's security model, while comprehensive, relies primarily on
   transport-layer encryption and broker-based access controls rather
   than end-to-end message encryption, which may not meet the security
   requirements of AI systems handling sensitive model data or
   proprietary algorithms.

3.6.  Next-Generation Agent Messaging: SLIM

   AGNTCY SLIM [SLIM] (Secure Low-Latency Interactive Messaging)
   represents a purpose-built protocol for modern agentic AI systems,
   designed to address the specific security, performance, and
   coordination requirements that existing protocols cannot fully
   satisfy.

   SLIM is intended as a transport layer for agent protocols like A2A
   [A2A], MCP [MCP], and ACP [ACP].  It handles secure routing, group
   messaging, and end-to-end encryption so protocol implementations can
   focus on agent semantics.  A registration-based model lets agents
   become reachable through the SLIM network without exposing server
   ports, while only routing nodes need to be publicly reachable.  This
   simplifies deployment for agents behind NATs and firewalls.

   SLIM carries Protocol Buffers messages over streaming gRPC [gRPC] on
   HTTP/2 or over WebSocket.  HTTP/2 supports multiplexed streams;
   latency, throughput, and connection costs depend on the
   implementation, network path, and workload and should be measured in
   the target deployment.

   SLIM is architected as a distributed system with a clear separation
   of concerns:

   *  *Data plane*: Routes messages across SLIM nodes using only
      metadata for efficient forwarding and topology management.

   *  *Session layer*: Provides reliable delivery, MLS-based end-to-end
      encryption, and secure group management (create, invite, join,
      remove).

   *  *Control plane*: Orchestrates routing nodes, configuration, and
      administrative operations.

   Routing nodes run only the data plane, keeping infrastructure
   lightweight, while language bindings include the data-plane client
   plus the session layer for full security and reliability.

   SLIM session delivery retries and acknowledgements are configurable
   and are not guaranteed for every session.  Applications should select
   and test the behavior they require; they should not infer exactly-
   once processing from transport retries.  Idempotent handlers and
   application-level deduplication remain useful when operations may be
   retried.

   SLIM separates hop-by-hop transport protection from optional session-
   layer content protection.  TLS may protect a transport hop.  When MLS
   is enabled for a session, application content is protected between
   session members while routing nodes forward it using message
   metadata.  Without MLS, transport security alone does not hide
   content from a routing node that terminates the protected hop.

   Authentication and authorization use configured identity providers
   and session policies.  MLS group membership and identity-provider
   credentials serve related but distinct purposes; deployments define
   how identity changes affect session membership.  Revoking an
   application credential does not by itself establish that a
   participant has been removed from an existing MLS group.

   SLIM's naming system is hierarchical and DID-inspired, for example:
   organization/namespace/service/instance.  This supports anycast
   routing (to any available instance), unicast routing (to a specific
   instance), and service discovery without hardcoded endpoints or
   external registries.  The structure maps cleanly to organizational
   boundaries and multi-tenant deployments.

   The protocol's support for both broker-based and peer-to-peer
   operation offers deployment flexibility.  While broker-based
   operation provides efficiency for multi-party group communications
   typical in agent coordination scenarios, peer-to-peer capabilities
   enable direct agent-to-agent communication when appropriate.  SLIM
   exposes two session types that map to common agent patterns: point-
   to-point sessions for tool calls and group sessions for coordination
   and broadcast.

   SLIM provides multi-language bindings.  Python and Go bindings are
   available today, with JavaScript/TypeScript, C#, and Kotlin in
   progress, enabling heterogeneous agent systems to interoperate on the
   same transport.

4.  Agent-Network Overlays

   Agent-network overlays combine service discovery, identity,
   authorization, and connectivity across networks.  They operate at the
   service-network layer and can work alongside messaging protocols and
   agent interaction protocols.

   Google Agent Mesh [AgentMesh] is a service-network overlay with a
   control plane, routers, and nodes [AgentMeshDocs].  The control plane
   admits nodes, issues credentials, distributes policy, and provides
   router configuration.  A node runs alongside an agent or service and
   connects that workload to the mesh.  Routers provide a directory for
   service discovery and relay traffic between nodes when a direct
   connection is unavailable.

4.1.  Enrollment and Identity

   A node can enroll using an OpenID Connect identity token or an
   operator-issued bootstrap token, and proves possession of its node
   key during enrollment.  The control plane issues a signed Biscuit
   credential containing the node identity and authorization facts, such
   as roles and identity-provider claims.  Nodes and routers verify
   these credentials using the control plane's public key.  The control
   plane also holds mesh policy and distributes it to the nodes that
   enforce that policy.

4.2.  Service Discovery and Calls

   Nodes publish services under typed names, including MCP, A2A, and
   inference services.  A node queries a router to discover the provider
   for a service name; routers maintain and exchange directory records.
   Nodes connect directly when network reachability permits, or use a
   router relay when it does not.  Agent Mesh uses libp2p for node
   connectivity, with QUIC or TCP transports; hole punching can
   establish a direct path when the network permits it.

   An agent uses the local node API rather than addressing remote nodes
   itself.  The API includes an MCP server for discovering and calling
   remote tools, an OpenAI-compatible endpoint for inference services,
   and proxy routes for individual services, including A2A agents.  For
   a service call, the connecting node authenticates to the provider
   with its credential.  The provider verifies the credential and
   evaluates the service policy before forwarding the request to its
   local backend.  The caller can also verify the provider's credential
   and attested labels before sending request data.

   The documented service-call path is point-to-point: a caller
   discovers one or more providers and addresses a call to a selected
   provider [AgentMeshNetworking].  Authorization policy can use
   identity groups when deciding which peer nodes a caller may reach,
   but such a group is not a multicast destination or shared message
   channel [AgentMeshAuthorization].  Routers also distribute signed
   mesh-control events through gossip; those events manage mesh state
   and are distinct from application messages.  The documented Agent
   Mesh APIs do not describe an application-level group messaging
   channel.

4.3.  Transport Security and Trust Boundaries

   Node-to-node connections use authenticated encrypted channels over
   libp2p.  The documented implementations use TLS 1.3 or Noise; routers
   relay encrypted traffic when they are on the path.  The control plane
   handles enrollment, credentials, and policy distribution, while
   application requests travel between nodes.  The control plane is the
   trust root for credentials and mesh policy; service nodes make the
   authorization decision for their hosted services.

   Agent Mesh describes service discovery, identity, authorization, and
   network connectivity.  Messaging protocols describe message delivery
   and session semantics.  The Agent Mesh node APIs expose MCP and A2A
   services to applications; this section describes that integration
   boundary without treating the overlay as a broker protocol.

5.  Security Analysis

   Security requirements for agentic AI systems extend well beyond the
   capabilities provided by traditional messaging protocols.  The
   autonomous nature of AI agents, combined with their access to
   sensitive data and computational resources, creates unique threat
   models that messaging infrastructure must address.

   *Post-Compromise Security*: MLS defines forward secrecy and post-
   compromise security properties for group key evolution.  These
   properties depend on correct MLS operation and group updates;
   recovery after a compromise is not automatic merely because MLS is
   enabled.  Deployments also need procedures to remove or re-
   authenticate compromised participants.

   *Post-Quantum Cryptography*: MLS does not by itself imply post-
   quantum protection.  The algorithms negotiated by an implementation
   determine whether a deployment has classical, hybrid, or post-quantum
   security.  The same applies to the other systems discussed here;
   deployments should verify the algorithms actually supported and
   enabled.

   *Multi-Domain Operations*: Agentic AI systems can span organizational
   and security domains.  Optional MLS protection can limit intermediary
   access to application content, but it does not replace identity
   federation, authorization, endpoint security, or operational trust
   decisions.

   *Dynamic Group Membership*: AI agent groups frequently change as
   agents join collaborations, complete tasks, or become unavailable.
   MLS's efficient group key management handles these membership changes
   while maintaining security properties, unlike approaches that require
   complete cryptographic context regeneration.

6.  Performance Characteristics

   The performance characteristics of messaging protocols significantly
   impact the behavior and capabilities of agentic AI systems,
   particularly as the number of agents and frequency of interactions
   scale.

   *Latency Sensitivity*: Many AI agent interactions are latency-
   sensitive.  SLIM supports streaming over HTTP/2 or WebSocket with
   Protocol Buffers encoding, but protocol choice alone does not
   determine end-to-end latency.  Implementations should benchmark
   representative payloads, fan-out patterns, and network paths.

   *Throughput Requirements*: Large-scale agentic AI systems may involve
   thousands of agents generating substantial message volumes.  While
   protocols like Kafka excel at raw throughput, they may introduce
   latency through their log-based architecture.  SLIM balances
   throughput and latency through efficient connection reuse and
   optional reliability levels.

   *Connection Efficiency*: Traditional protocols often require separate
   connections for each communication pattern or security context.
   SLIM's connection multiplexing allows a single HTTP/2 connection to
   handle diverse communication patterns between agents, reducing
   resource consumption and connection establishment overhead.

   *Streaming Performance*: AI agents frequently exchange streaming
   data—such as token-by-token language model outputs or real-time
   sensor data.  SLIM's native gRPC streaming support over HTTP/2
   provides efficient bidirectional streaming without the overhead of
   connection-per-stream approaches.

7.  Deployment and Operational Considerations

   The operational characteristics of messaging protocols significantly
   impact the total cost of ownership and operational complexity of
   agentic AI systems.

   *Infrastructure Requirements*: Traditional enterprise protocols like
   AMQP require broker infrastructure, while Kafka deployments typically
   use a distributed cluster.  SLIM deployments use a network of routing
   nodes; the required topology depends on deployment scale and
   availability goals.

   *Monitoring and Observability*: Debugging distributed agentic AI
   systems requires comprehensive visibility into agent communications.
   SLIM's foundation on standard HTTP/2 infrastructure enables use of
   existing observability tools and practices, while proprietary
   protocols may require specialized monitoring solutions.

   *Integration with Cloud Services*: Modern AI deployments increasingly
   rely on cloud services for scalability and managed operations.
   SLIM's HTTP/2 foundation integrates naturally with cloud load
   balancers, API gateways, and observability services, while
   specialized messaging protocols may require additional integration
   layers.

   *Compliance and Auditing*: Regulated deployments need explicit audit,
   retention, and access-control policies.  SLIM's optional session-
   state persistence is not a message archive or audit log, and
   encryption alone does not establish regulatory compliance.

8.  Connectivity for Agents Behind NAT

   Messaging semantics and network reachability are separate concerns.
   An agent that cannot accept inbound connections can still participate
   as a client when it can establish an outbound connection to a
   reachable broker or routing node.  The established connection carries
   traffic in both directions, so replies and subscribed messages can
   return without opening an inbound firewall port at the agent.  Long-
   lived connections require suitable keepalive settings and network
   policy that permits egress to the service endpoint.

   This client-connection pattern does not, by itself, make a private
   agent service directly reachable by another agent.  For direct RPC to
   a service behind NAT, deployments need a reachable gateway, relay,
   reverse connection, or network overlay.  Alternatively, the private
   agent can subscribe to requests on a broker and publish replies over
   its existing outbound connection.  The latter uses the messaging
   system's request/reply conventions and requires application logic for
   correlation, timeouts, retries, and authorization.

8.1.  Patterns by System

   *AMQP*: An AMQP client initiates a TCP connection to an AMQP listener
   on a broker.  An agent in a private network can connect outward to a
   broker reachable from that network; the broker mediates communication
   with other connected clients.  Where the broker is private as well,
   network routing, a VPN, or an implementation-specific broker bridge
   is needed between the agent network and the broker network.  RabbitMQ
   documents separate client listeners and inter-node ports; cluster
   inter-node access is an operator networking concern and is distinct
   from client connectivity [RabbitMQNetworking].

   *MQTT*: MQTT defines the client as opening a bidirectional connection
   to a server, then publishing and subscribing through that connection
   [MQTT].  This fits devices or agents behind NAT when they can reach
   the broker outbound.  MQTT 5.0 supports TCP-based transports and
   WebSocket; a broker's WebSocket listener can be used by browser
   clients, subject to the broker implementation and its network
   configuration [RabbitMQWebMQTT].  A local MQTT broker or bridge can
   aggregate clients on a private network and forward configured topic
   traffic to an upstream broker.

   *NATS*: Clients connect to a NATS server.  For an edge network that
   permits outbound connections only, a local NATS leaf node can dial a
   hub and bridge subject interest and matching messages in both
   directions over that link.  Local agents then connect to the leaf on
   the private network; they do not each need a public address or a
   direct connection to the hub [NATSLeaf].  NATS gateways connect
   separate NATS clusters, while leaf nodes connect edge servers to a
   hub [NATSGateway].

   *Kafka*: A Kafka client can initiate its connections outbound, but
   connecting to the bootstrap address is not sufficient if broker
   metadata advertises addresses the client cannot reach.  Every broker
   address returned through advertised.listeners must be reachable from
   the agent's network, directly or through configured network paths
   such as private routing or a VPN [KafkaListeners].  Kafka's broker
   topology is therefore an explicit part of the connectivity design for
   clients in separate private networks.

   *AMQP over WebSockets*: A WebSocket binding can let browser-based
   clients connect through networks that permit HTTP(S) egress, when the
   chosen broker or gateway implements that binding.  WebSockets change
   how the client reaches the service; they do not remove the need for a
   reachable broker endpoint or provide an inbound route to a private
   agent.

   *SLIM*: A SLIM endpoint can initiate a streaming gRPC connection over
   HTTP/2 or a WebSocket connection to an accessible routing node.
   Messages are then routed by name through the interconnected routing-
   node network, rather than requiring every application endpoint to
   expose an Internet-reachable listener.  Deployments still need
   reachable routing nodes and connectivity between the routing nodes
   that carry the route.

   *Agent Mesh*: An Agent Mesh node runs beside an agent or service and
   connects to routers; nodes can communicate directly when network
   conditions permit and use router relays otherwise.  This lets a node
   behind NAT participate without publishing its own reachable service
   address.  Its service discovery and authorization behavior is
   described in the Agent-Network Overlays section
   [AgentMeshNetworking].

   These patterns place a reachable broker, edge bridge, gateway, or
   router on the network boundary and keep end-device connectivity
   outbound.  They differ in what that boundary component provides:
   message brokering, topic or subject bridging, log access, or name-
   routed service connectivity.  Deployments should also account for the
   trust boundary at that component, connection loss and reconnection
   behavior, and whether messages are buffered while an endpoint is
   offline.

9.  Agent Gateways for Inspection

   An Agent Gateway can enforce content policy when it is placed inline
   on a request path and receives the application protocol in a form it
   can parse.  For example, a gateway between an agent and an MCP
   server, A2A agent, or LLM API can inspect requests and responses,
   apply authorization and content guardrails, and forward permitted
   traffic to a backend.  Agent Gateway documents HTTP and gRPC
   proxying, native MCP and A2A routing, and request/response guardrails
   for those protocols [AgentGateway] [AgentGatewayMCP]
   [AgentGatewayLLM] [AgentGatewayA2A].

   For inspection, the gateway must be an actual data-path hop.  TLS
   therefore terminates at the gateway, which establishes a separate
   protected connection to the upstream service.  This makes the gateway
   a trusted plaintext-handling component; deployment policy should
   cover credential forwarding, payload logging, redaction, audit
   retention, and failure behavior.  A generic TCP proxy can forward a
   connection, but application-level inspection requires a protocol-
   aware gateway or adapter.

9.1.  Synchronous Enforcement and Streaming

   Content enforcement is an inline decision, not a passive copy of
   traffic.  For a request-phase check, the gateway receives and parses
   the request, evaluates the policy locally or calls a policy service,
   and holds the request until it has a decision.  It then forwards the
   original or modified request, or rejects it without contacting the
   backend.  A response-phase check runs after the backend returns and
   before the gateway releases the response to the client.  Agent
   Gateway documents request and response phases for MCP guardrail
   processors, including pass, mutate, and deny outcomes
   [AgentGatewayMCP].

   This introduces enforcement latency and may require buffering the
   content being checked.  Local deterministic rules can reduce this
   delay; remote classifiers or policy services add their processing and
   network time.  Deployments should set body-size limits, timeouts,
   concurrency limits, and an explicit failure mode.  Fail-closed
   behavior preserves the policy when the checker is unavailable but can
   make the service unavailable; fail-open behavior preserves
   availability but allows unchecked traffic.  Audit-only evaluation can
   help tune policies, but it does not block or redact content.

   Streaming makes response enforcement different from request
   enforcement.  A gateway can hold a complete buffered response,
   inspect or redact it, and then release it, at the cost of delaying
   the first response byte and storing the response while it is checked.
   Alternatively, it can inspect a response stream incrementally, but
   bytes already sent cannot be retracted.  Agent Gateway's LLM
   guardrail documentation states that streamed responses can be
   rejected when a guardrail matches, but response masking does not
   apply to content already sent; buffered responses support masking
   [AgentGatewayLLM].  Applications that require redaction before any
   content reaches the caller therefore need buffering or an endpoint-
   side policy, rather than relying on post-send inspection.

   The integration depends on the messaging system:

   *  *HTTP-based A2A, MCP, or model APIs*: Configure clients or service
      routing to send requests through the gateway.  The gateway can
      apply protocol-aware policy before forwarding to a public or
      privately reachable backend.

   *  *AMQP, MQTT, NATS, or Kafka*: A gateway can be placed at the
      client/broker boundary, but a generic TCP listener does not
      interpret broker frames or message payloads.  Prompt inspection
      therefore requires a protocol-aware proxy, broker extension, or
      application adapter.  An alternative is to inspect messages at the
      producer before publishing and at the consumer after delivery.

   *  *SLIM*: A generic gateway attached to a routing node does not
      parse SLIM's Protocol Buffers message envelope.  Relay inspection
      requires a SLIM-aware integration.  If MLS is enabled, application
      content remains opaque to routing nodes; inspect before MLS
      encryption at the sending endpoint, after decryption at the
      receiving endpoint, or make the inspection gateway an explicitly
      trusted session participant.

   *  *Agent Mesh*: Agent Mesh documents an integration with Agent
      Gateway in front of an agentmesh-node [AgentMeshAgentGateway].
      The client sends its identity-provider token to Agent Gateway.
      The gateway validates the token, exchanges it at the node's
      /oauth/token endpoint for a caller-bound mesh credential, then
      forwards the request to the node's /mesh/<peer-id>/<type>/<name>/
      proxy with that credential.  The node connects to the selected
      mesh provider, and the provider node enforces its service policy.
      This places supported application-layer checks at Agent Gateway
      before the request enters the mesh and before the response returns
      to the client.

      The gateway is not inserted into the Agent Mesh router's relay or
      peer-link path.  Those links carry authenticated encrypted
      traffic, whether relayed or direct, and are not readable by a
      gateway attached only to a router.  To make gateway policy
      mandatory, clients must use the gateway-facing endpoint rather
      than bypassing it and calling a node API directly.

   Colocating an Agent Gateway with a relay does not by itself enable
   inspection: the gateway must terminate or receive the application-
   level plaintext, and the system must route every applicable path
   through it.  If end-to-end encryption is required, inspection belongs
   at a trusted endpoint or authorized group member; adding an
   intermediary that can decrypt changes the trust model.

10.  RPC in Agentic Protocols and Relationship to Messaging

   Agentic AI systems are predominantly built around Remote Procedure
   Call (RPC)-oriented protocols.  A2A [A2A], the Model Context Protocol
   (MCP) [MCP], and the Agent Client Protocol (ACP) [ACP] all expose
   synchronous request/response semantics: a caller issues a structured
   request, awaits a response within a bounded time, and receives an
   explicit result or error.  This design provides well-defined
   interfaces, typed contracts, and composable error handling that make
   agents discoverable and interoperable across heterogeneous systems.

   However, pure point-to-point RPC does not scale to all coordination
   demands of multi-agent systems.  Fan-out invocations, asynchronous
   event delivery, streaming responses, dynamic group membership, and
   multi-domain security are capabilities that RPC protocols delegate to
   the underlying transport.  This section examines how RPC and
   messaging interoperate in agentic systems, and how SLIM's SRPC
   capability provides a purpose-built solution.

10.1.  Agentic Protocols Are RPC-Oriented

   The three leading agentic coordination protocols each define an RPC-
   based interaction model:

   *Model Context Protocol (MCP)* defines a JSON-RPC interface between
   LLM hosts and tools or resources.  A host invokes tools by name with
   typed parameters and receives typed results or streaming token
   outputs.  MCP is designed for synchronous tool calls with optional
   server-side streaming for partial results.

   *Agent2Agent (A2A)* is an HTTP-based protocol for delegating tasks
   between agents.  An orchestrator submits task requests to worker
   agents and receives structured results or a stream of progress
   events.  Agent capabilities are advertised via Agent Cards, enabling
   dynamic discovery without pre-configuration.

   *Agent Client Protocol (ACP)* standardizes JSON-RPC communication
   between code editors and coding agents, operating over stdio for
   local agents or HTTP and WebSocket for remote agents.  It enables
   editors to invoke coding agents and receive structured responses,
   following a pattern analogous to the Language Server Protocol for
   language tools.

   All three share a fundamental structure: a caller invokes a named
   callee with parameters and awaits a response within a bounded time.
   SLIM is designed to serve as the transport layer beneath all three.

10.2.  RPC vs. Messaging: Synchronous vs. Asynchronous

   *  *RPC (A2A, MCP, ACP)*: The caller issues a request and blocks or
      awaits a timely response.  Semantics emphasize tightly scoped
      operations with bounded latency and explicit error contracts.

   *  *Messaging (AMQP, MQTT, NATS, Kafka, SLIM)*: Decoupled producers
      and consumers communicate via topics, subjects, or queues.
      Delivery can be one-to-one, one-to-many, or many-to-many, with
      loose coupling, buffering, and retries.  Producers are not
      inherently blocked by consumers.

   In practice, agentic applications need both: synchronous tool
   invocations for interactivity and asynchronous channels for streaming
   output, progress, coordination, and fan-out/fan-in patterns.

10.3.  Challenges of Running RPC over Messaging

   Layering RPC semantics onto an asynchronous messaging substrate
   introduces several non-trivial challenges:

   *  *Request/response correlation*: Messaging systems are decoupled by
      design and provide no direct return path.  Implementing RPC
      requires correlating requests and responses using unique
      identifiers and temporary reply channels.

   *  *Latency and ordering*: Messaging layers may introduce variable
      delivery latency and do not guarantee strict ordering, which can
      conflict with synchronous RPC contracts.

   *  *Error propagation*: Messaging systems may buffer, retry, or drop
      messages, making it difficult to propagate errors and timeouts in
      a way that matches RPC error contracts.

   *  *Streaming and multiplexing*: Supporting streaming RPC (server
      streaming, client streaming, bidirectional) over messaging
      requires careful management of stream lifecycles, backpressure,
      and multiplexing multiple logical RPCs over shared channels.

   *  *Security and authorization*: Ensuring that only authorized
      callers can invoke specific methods, and that all messages are
      authenticated and encrypted end-to-end, is more complex in a
      distributed, group-based messaging environment.

   SRPC resolves all of these challenges natively, as described below.

10.4.  When Asynchronous Feels Synchronous

   Asynchronous transports can provide an interactive, RPC-like
   experience when:

   *  A request message carries a correlation ID and a reply-to
      destination.

   *  The callee publishes a response to the reply destination within a
      short SLA.

   *  Client libraries surface responses as futures or promises and
      manage timeouts and retries.

   This pattern underpins agent UIs where a user triggers an action and
   expects prompt, possibly streaming, results.

10.5.  Bridging Patterns: RPC over Messaging

   *  *Request/Reply over Pub/Sub*: Implement RPC by publishing a
      command event and awaiting a correlated reply event (applicable to
      AMQP, NATS, MQTT, and SLIM topics).

   *  *Streaming RPC*: Use bidirectional streams (gRPC over SLIM
      HTTP/2/3) to deliver token streams, partial results, or progress
      updates while retaining an RPC caller experience.

   *  *Sagas and CQRS*: For multi-step workflows across agents,
      coordinate via asynchronous orchestration with idempotency keys,
      correlation/causation IDs, and compensating transactions.

   *  *Backpressure and Flow Control*: Prefer streaming transports
      (HTTP/2/3, gRPC) or messaging systems with flow control when
      returning large or continuous results.

10.6.  SLIM RPC (SRPC)

   SRPC layers request/response semantics directly onto SLIM's secure
   messaging fabric, addressing all of the challenges enumerated above:

   *  *Correlation and reply routing*: SRPC manages correlation IDs and
      reply channel lifecycle automatically, with no application-level
      plumbing required.

   *  *Idempotency and deduplication*: SRPC idempotency keys make
      retries safe without duplicating side effects.

   *  *Ordering and synchronization*: Lightweight ordering guarantees
      for both request/response and streaming patterns.

   *  *All four gRPC interaction patterns*: Unary, server streaming,
      client streaming, and bidirectional streaming are all supported,
      enabling direct mapping of A2A, MCP, and ACP interaction models
      onto SLIM.

   *  *Integrated security*: Every SRPC call inherits SLIM's MLS end-to-
      end encryption and OAuth-based authorization without additional
      configuration.

   See the SRPC specification for details [SRPC].

10.7.  Advantages of SLIM for Agentic Protocols

   SLIM augments the point-to-point RPC model of A2A, MCP, and ACP with
   capabilities that are difficult to achieve over plain request/
   response transports:

   *  *Scatter-gather RPC*: Invoke a single RPC across many agents
      simultaneously (by topic, group, or label) and aggregate responses
      (first-success, quorum, or all-success) using correlation IDs.

   *  *Group addressing and dynamic membership*: Target MLS-secured
      groups; add or remove agents without reconfiguring endpoints or
      updating caller code.

   *  *Streaming responses*: Return partial results or token streams
      from each agent over a single multiplexed connection.

   *  *Idempotency and safe retries*: SRPC idempotency keys enable
      robust retry without duplicating effects—critical for expensive or
      stateful agent operations.

   *  *QoS, deadlines, and backpressure*: Apply delivery guarantees,
      per-call timeouts, and flow control to avoid overload while
      maintaining interactivity.

   *  *End-to-end security*: MLS encryption and OAuth-based policy apply
      uniformly across both RPC and messaging channels.

   *  *Observability*: Correlation and causation IDs, combined with
      standard HTTP/2 transport, enable distributed tracing and per-
      agent metrics with existing tooling.

   These capabilities let A2A, MCP, and ACP interactions scale beyond
   one-to-one invocations, enabling broadcast queries, coordinated
   multi-agent actions, and efficient collection of results in real
   time.

10.8.  Security Implications

   *  *Unified identity*: OAuth tokens are reused across RPC calls and
      messaging channels for consistent policy enforcement.  An agent's
      identity and authorization scope apply uniformly to tool calls and
      subscription channels.

   *  *End-to-end encryption*: MLS-backed channels ensure that RPC
      requests, responses, and streaming payloads are encrypted end-to-
      end, independently of transport hops.  Routing nodes cannot
      inspect RPC call content.

   *  *Caller authentication*: SRPC carries MLS group membership proofs
      with each call, allowing callees to verify that the caller is an
      authorized group member before executing the requested operation.

10.9.  Guidance: When to Choose What

   *  Use *RPC (A2A, MCP, ACP)* for low-latency point operations with
      immediate feedback and well-defined error contracts.

   *  Use *Messaging* for broadcast/fan-out, decoupling, retries,
      buffering, and multi-party coordination.

   *  Use *SRPC over SLIM* for any of the above when end-to-end
      security, scatter-gather invocation, streaming responses, or
      multi-organization deployment is required.

12.  Security Considerations

   This document is an informational comparison of messaging protocols
   for agentic AI systems.  Security properties specific to each
   protocol are discussed in the Security Analysis section of this
   document.  Readers deploying any of the protocols described SHOULD
   consult the security considerations sections of the respective
   protocol specifications.

   Among the systems analyzed, SLIM explicitly integrates optional MLS-
   based end-to-end protection for session content.  The other protocols
   and systems do not provide that same integrated MLS model, although
   applications can add message-level encryption or other end-to-end
   protections.  Transport-layer TLS alone protects a hop and does not
   hide content from an intermediary that terminates that hop.
